Dear Hiring Manager,

I am writing to apply for the Graduate Engineer position at Industrus Engineering. As a final-year Mechatronics Engineering student at UTS with hands-on experience in robotics, computer vision, and embedded systems, I am particularly drawn to Industrus’s work on projects like the lithium-ion battery storage system and the engineered timber office building – the kind of technically diverse, high-impact work I want to grow into as a graduate engineer.

A commitment to ethical conduct and the highest standards of professional accountability (3.1)

On the way to a field trial for an agricultural computer vision system I had developed during my internship at the UTS Robotics Institute, I raised a concern with my supervisor about whether our system could displace the livelihoods of the farmers we were about to visit. I was determined to understand the ethical implications of the technology before presenting it to real stakeholders. My supervisor clarified that the system does not make decisions on behalf of farmers, but rather provides them with higher-accuracy, lower-effort information about animal traits – augmenting rather than replacing their judgement. Hearing this, I was able to engage with the trial participants honestly and confidently. I believe that raising the concern proactively, rather than waiting for someone else to notice it, reflects the kind of accountability I bring to engineering work.

Demonstrated ability to effectively communicate both with other engineers and with stakeholders from different fields (3.2)

At the UTS Tech Festival, I presented my robotics project to an audience that ranged from academics and technical staff to general guests with little or no background in robotics. My task was to make the work engaging and accessible to every person who approached, regardless of their technical level. For non-technical visitors, I set aside jargon and focused on what the system could see and do in plain terms, using live demonstrations to hold attention. For technical audiences, I shifted into depth on the perception pipeline and hardware stack. The result was a continuous flow of interested visitors throughout the event, and several academics approached me afterwards to discuss the research further.

The ability to engage with a creative, innovative and proactive environment (3.3)

In a team project, we were given a deliberately open brief: build a step tracker using an accelerometer. With no further specifications, my team treated this as a product design challenge rather than a circuit exercise. I took ownership of the wireless integration layer and enclosure design – making the device fully wearable with a forearm strap, engineering the battery to last over eight hours of continuous use, integrating status LEDs bright enough to read outdoors, adding Bluetooth connectivity to a BLE heart rate monitor, and building a visually appealing GUI – none of which was required. The result was a polished, consumer-grade wearable that went to UTS Tech Festival, where it drew significant interest. I believe this project reflects how I approach open-ended problems: the minimum viable answer is rarely the most valuable one.

Demonstrated ability to use and manage information (3.4)

During my internship at the UTS Robotics Institute, I was responsible for preparing and managing a large proprietary dataset of over 100GB of visual agricultural data, held under strict NDA. My task was to curate, preprocess, and label this data for training a convolutional neural network, while ensuring it remained secure and handled in accordance with the confidentiality agreement at all times. I established a structured local pipeline for data organisation, labelling workflows, and version control, allowing me to process the dataset systematically without risking accidental exposure or loss. The resulting dataset underpinned model training and evaluation that directly fed into field trial outcomes.

The ability to manage your own performance in a professional environment (3.5)

As the sole intern on my project at the UTS Robotics Institute, I was given full ownership of the computer vision and perception pipeline from day one – more autonomy than I had expected. To manage this effectively, I kept detailed logs of my daily progress and decisions, and presented updates at weekly team meetings where I reported on completed work, blockers, and next steps. This discipline helped me stay on track independently while keeping my supervisor and the broader research team informed. Over the course of the internship, I optimised inference latency from approximately 300ms to 12ms and successfully validated the system in live field conditions – outcomes I attribute in large part to the structured self-management approach I maintained throughout.

A demonstrated ability to work as part of a team and to show leadership when required (3.6)

In a team of three, I co-developed a robotic portrait-drawing system using a UR3 arm and ROS2, a project that became one of the standout submissions at our faculty showcase. When our progress stalled mid-project due to competing priorities and unresolved technical decisions, I took the initiative to restructure our approach – clarifying ownership of each subsystem, establishing a shared progress tracker, and running regular check-ins to surface blockers early. I also took responsibility for the stroke planning and path output pipeline, one of the more technically demanding components. The project was completed on time and to a standard that surprised both our assessors and the audience – a result that came from the team committing to a shared plan and holding each other accountable.
